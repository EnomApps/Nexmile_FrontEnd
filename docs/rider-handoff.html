Rider App
Design Handoff

The customer app now has a defined design system. This is what the rider app should take from it, what it should deliberately do differently, and the questions worth settling before either team builds further.

Start here: the two apps are not the same job

The instinct is to copy the customer app so the products match. Resist half of that. A customer browses indoors, two hands, deciding what to eat. A rider glances at a phone in sunlight, one hand, often beside a running bike, and needs one number and one button.

Share

Colour, typography, corner radii, motion timings, icon style, the error and empty-state patterns. These make the two apps feel like one company.

Diverge

Density, tap-target size, contrast, and how much motion is used. These follow the context of use, and the rider's is harsher.

Worth copying, not just the hex values The neutrals are green-shifted, not grey — ink is #14170F, not #141414. It is subtle and it is why the dark theme reads as Nexmile rather than as a default Material app. Ask the rider team to use the same neutrals rather than picking their own greys.

Deliberately different for riders

These are the places where copying the customer app would make the rider app worse.

Tap targets

The customer app uses 38–48px controls. Accept, Pickup and Deliver should be no smaller than 64px tall, full width, with real separation between them. A mis-tap on /deliver is not recoverable in the way a mis-tap on a menu item is.

Sunlight

A rider reads the screen outdoors. The customer app's muted body text (#5C6356) is fine indoors and marginal in daylight. Push the rider app's body text to full ink, and reserve muted grey for genuinely secondary information.

Motion

Skip staggered list reveals on the available-orders screen. A customer enjoys a list arriving; a rider racing another rider to accept a job does not. Keep press feedback and state transitions; drop the choreography.

One state above all

on_duty / off_duty is the most consequential thing on the rider's screen — it decides whether they earn. It should be readable across a room and impossible to toggle by accident. This has no equivalent in the customer app, so there is no pattern to copy; it needs designing on its own terms.

What to ask the rider team

Ordered by how expensive they get to change later.

  1. Are you using the same colour and type tokens?

    If they have already picked their own greens or greys, that is the one thing worth fixing immediately — it is cheap now and everywhere later.

  2. How many languages does the rider app support?

    The customer app ships 23, fully translated. If the rider app ships 3, that is a defensible decision — riders are a smaller, more uniform group — but it should be a decision, not an accident. Ask which they chose and why.

  3. Does the KYC wizard save progress between steps?

    Six documents plus eleven detail fields is a long flow to lose. The API has separate /kyc/details and /kyc/documents endpoints, so partial saving is possible — confirm they are using it rather than submitting at the end.

  4. What happens visually when a rider is pending approval?

    A rider sits in pending until an admin approves them, and cannot work. That waiting state needs a real screen, not a disabled button — otherwise support gets the call.

  5. How often are you posting location, and is that visible?

    The customer app shows the rider's live position on a map. That only works if /rider/location is being posted on a sensible interval. Agree the cadence between both teams, and show the rider that it is on — silent battery use is how riders learn to force-quit the app.

  6. Are order statuses shown with the server's own labels?

    The customer app renders status_label verbatim, so a status the build predates degrades instead of crashing. If both apps do this, a new status can ship server-side without releasing either app.

  7. Is the release / cancel path designed as carefully as accept?

    Releasing an order is the rider's escape hatch when something goes wrong on the road. It tends to get built as an afterthought and is the thing they need most under pressure.

Two things to warn them about

Field names do not match the schema Building the customer app against the live API turned up cases where the documented field name and the sent field name differ — cart lines arrive as cart_item_id where the schema says id, and one list endpoint sends its items rather than a count. Tell the rider team to log real responses early rather than trusting the schema; we lost time to exactly this.

Numbers arrive as strings Prices, booleans and IDs come back as JSON strings in some places and numbers in others. Every value should be coerced on the way in. A rider app that crashes on "49.97" mid-delivery is a support call and a lost order.
